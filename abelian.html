Same fusion rules, different anyons

Fusion specifies the total excitation type of a group; the vacuum type, written \(1\), means no remaining topological excitation. An Abelian anyon theory has one fusion outcome for each pair of types. A braid records their transport and exchanges, up to smooth deformations avoiding collisions; in an Abelian theory it multiplies the state by a scalar phase. In the toric-code spin model, the types \(e\) and \(m\) each exchange with factor \(+1\), yet taking one around the other gives \(-1\). I therefore need more than a self-exchange angle: I need fusion, exchanges, and windings involving different types. A symmetric, nonsingular integer matrix \(K\) organizes these data for the Abelian models studied here.

Let \(K\) be an \(N\times N\) matrix. Each particle is described by an integer column label \(\ell\in\mathbb Z^N\); write \(\ell'\) for a second particle’s label. Adding labels describes fusion. Labels differing by \(Kn\), where \(n\in\mathbb Z^N\), differ by an attached particle that can be created locally. They belong to the same topological sector: the same excitation type after identifying such local attachments. Write \([\ell]\) for an equivalence class and \(\mathcal A\) for their fusion group:

\[\ell\sim\ell+Kn,\qquad [\ell]\times[\ell']=[\ell+\ell'],\qquad \mathcal A=\mathbb Z^N/K\mathbb Z^N.\]

Brackets mean an equivalence class, not a quantum state. In this description a local particle has full-winding factor 1 with every anyon, but its own exchange factor may be \(-1\): it need not be a boson. The toric-code and double-semion models have the same four-sector fusion group but different exchange factors. A semion has exchange factor \(i\); its mirror partner has \(-i\). The models follow Lu and Vishwanath, Sec. II A, Eq. (25), and Appendix C.

The table uses \(q_\ell\) for electric charge and \(e>0\) for its reference unit; this charge unit is distinct from the toric-code particle named \(e\). The integer vector \(t\) specifies coupling to electric charge, and \(\nu=t^{\mathsf T}K^{-1}t\) is the dimensionless Hall-response coefficient, specified below. The number of sectors is \(|\det K|\). The two Laughlin examples have \(K=(2)\) and \(K=(3)\): their local unit-charge particles are respectively bosonic and fermionic. They model fractional quantum Hall phases in two dimensions: excitations in the bulk (the sample interior) require a nonzero minimum energy, and the electrical response transverse to an applied field is quantized.

Why the formulas fit together

The effective model assigns internal fluxes to an excitation. For a target labeled \(\ell'\), solve \(Kx=\ell'\); the entries of \(2\pi x\) are the fluxes of the model's internal fields, not the external magnetic field. A probe labeled \(\ell\) couples to these fields and acquires phase \(2\pi\ell^{\mathsf T}x\) on a full counterclockwise circuit. This is analogous to the Aharonov–Bohm phase acquired by a charge encircling magnetic flux. It is physical input to the effective description, not a proof that a microscopic Hamiltonian realizes it. Write \(M_{\ell,\ell'}\) for the full-winding factor and \(T_\ell\) for the identical-particle exchange factor in the stated convention. The K-matrix construction in Wen’s review, Sec. II, develops their field-theory origin. Solving the linear system gives

\[M_{\ell,\ell'}=e^{2\pi i\ell^{\mathsf T}K^{-1}\ell'},\qquad T_\ell=e^{i\pi\ell^{\mathsf T}K^{-1}\ell}.\]

These formulas use the positive, counterclockwise convention. Reversing a path conjugates its phase. \(T_\ell\) is also the topological twist, the phase for a \(2\pi\) self-rotation; it is often called topological spin. For these Abelian representatives it equals the identical-particle exchange factor. It is a complex number; an exchange angle \(\theta_\ell\) instead satisfies \(T_\ell=e^{i\theta_\ell}\). The full self-winding is \(M_{\ell,\ell}=T_\ell^2\). The exchange formula is part of the specified model: knowing only the full winding would leave a sign ambiguity.

Why can two bosons fuse to a fermion?

Expand the quadratic form for the composite. Its two cross terms give

\[T_{\ell+\ell'}=T_\ell T_{\ell'}M_{\ell,\ell'}.\]

Thus toric-code \(e\) and \(m\) have \(T_e=T_m=1\), but \(M_{e,m}=-1\), so \(T_{e+m}=-1\). In double semion, \(T_s=i\) and \(T_{\bar s}=-i\), while \(M_{s,\bar s}=1\); their composite \(b\) is bosonic. Both models have fusion group \(\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2\), but their exchange data distinguish the phases.

What changes when a local particle is attached?

Substitute \(\ell+Kn\) directly into the formulas:

\[\frac{M_{\ell+Kn,\ell'}}{M_{\ell,\ell'}}=e^{2\pi i n^{\mathsf T}\ell'}=1,\qquad \frac{T_{\ell+Kn}}{T_\ell}=e^{i\pi n^{\mathsf T}Kn}.\]

If every diagonal entry of \(K\) is even, the last exponent is an even multiple of \(i\pi\): the local particles are bosons. For \(K=3\), attaching one electron gives a minus sign. The sector’s mutual windings and charge modulo \(e\) survive electron attachment, while its displayed exchange factor changes. Keeping the electron as a distinct object would require a different bookkeeping convention; this note explicitly quotients by all local particles.

Charge requires another piece of information

The matrix \(K\) specifies statistics, but it does not identify electromagnetic charge. An integer charge vector \(t\) supplies that information. Let \(q_\ell\) be the charge of label \(\ell\), \(e>0\) the charge unit, and \(h\) Planck's constant. Define the Hall conductivity \(\sigma_{xy}\) by \(j_x=\sigma_{xy}E_y\) with \(E_x=0\), where \(j_x\) is electric current per transverse length and \(E_y\) is the applied electric field. Its dimensionless coefficient is \(\nu\):

\[\frac{q_\ell}{e}=t^{\mathsf T}K^{-1}\ell,\qquad \nu=t^{\mathsf T}K^{-1}t,\qquad \sigma_{xy}=\nu\frac{e^2}{h}.\]

Positive circuits are counterclockwise when viewed from \(+z\). The positive Laughlin \(K\) used here specifies one chirality, or choice of handedness for the phase and its transverse response. We choose \(t=0\) for the two neutral models and \(t=1\) for the Laughlin models. For electronic \(K=3\), \(\ell=1\) is a \(+e/3\) quasihole, an excitation corresponding to a local deficit of electron charge. The local label \(\ell=-3\) is an electron of charge \(-e\). Attaching it sends \(1\to-2\), changing the charge to \(-2e/3\), multiplying the exchange factor by \(-1\), and preserving every mutual winding. These charge and response formulas are given in Lu and Vishwanath, Sec. II A, Eq. (9).

A mirror theory can be represented by \(K\to-K\), \(t\to-t\) while keeping the integer labels: the charges stay fixed, the exchange and winding factors are conjugated, and \(\nu\) and the net edge chirality change sign.

Charge adds exactly for integer labels; reducing a fusion result to its listed representative can remove a local integer charge. Consequently the charge of a sector is specified modulo \(e\). Equal charge does not generally imply equal statistics: in the neutral toric example all four charges vanish, while their exchanges and windings differ.

What the count tells us

The columns of \(K\) generate the sublattice of local-particle labels. Its fundamental cell contains \(|\det K|\) inequivalent integer labels. This gives the number of sectors and the ideal ground-state degeneracy on a closed torus. The description assumes a nonzero bulk excitation gap and a system much larger than its correlation length, the distance over which connected local correlations decay. A generic finite sample can have a small splitting between these ground states. A disk with edges is a different problem. See Wen, Secs. I–II.

An edge mode is a low-energy collective excitation propagating along the boundary. In the standard edge description of these Abelian models, the signature of \(K\)—its number of positive eigenvalues minus its number of negative eigenvalues—gives the chiral central charge \(c_-\). Each elementary chiral boson mode in this description contributes one, with sign set by its propagation direction. Thus \(c_-\) measures the net chirality and controls the thermal Hall response, transverse heat transport driven by a temperature gradient: it is \(1\) for either displayed Laughlin model and \(0\) for toric code and double semion. Zero net chirality does not require a gapless boundary; counterpropagating modes can sometimes be gapped by interactions. See Lu and Vishwanath, Sec. II A.

This notebook specifies long-distance topological data. It does not calculate a gap, establish a phase from a microscopic Hamiltonian, or predict an experimental interference visibility. The integer-label addition used here has one fusion outcome per pair; it cannot describe the non-Abelian fusion spaces in the Ising and Fibonacci notes.
