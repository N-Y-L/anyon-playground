One state, two fusion bases

A topological charge labels an excitation type or the total type of a group; it need not be electric charge. The Fibonacci anyon model has types \(1\), meaning vacuum, and \(\tau\), with fusion rules \(1\times\tau=\tau\) and \(\tau\times\tau=1+\tau\). The plus sign lists the possible total types of a pair, called its fusion channels; it does not assign probabilities. I want to calculate an exchange by resolving the pair into these channels, each of which acquires its own phase.

Take three \(\tau\) anyons with fixed total charge \(\tau\). The first pair may have charge \(1\) or \(\tau\); either intermediate charge can combine with the third anyon to give total \(\tau\). There are therefore two independent states. We may instead resolve the last pair first. These choices define two orthonormal fusion bases of the same two-dimensional space, much as coupling three angular momenta in different orders changes the intermediate label. Parentheses specify which pair is combined; their subscripts give its total charge. With \(a\) and \(b\) the first-pair and last-pair charges, respectively,

\[|a\rangle_L=|((\tau\tau)_a\tau)_\tau\rangle,\qquad |b\rangle_R=|(\tau(\tau\tau)_b)_\tau\rangle,\qquad a,b\in\{1,\tau\}.\]

Let \(c_L\) and \(c_R\) be the column vectors of complex amplitudes for a normalized state in the left and right bases, each in channel order \((1,\tau)\). Their squared component magnitudes are the corresponding pair-charge probabilities. A choice of phases for the basis vectors is sometimes called a gauge; here this has nothing to do with changing an electromagnetic field. In the convention used here the basis-change matrix \(F\) is real, symmetric, and unitary. Write \(\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2\) for the golden ratio:

\[c_R=Fc_L,\qquad F=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi^{-1}&\varphi^{-1/2}\\\varphi^{-1/2}&-\varphi^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\qquad \varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2},\qquad F^{-1}=F.\]

This F-move is a passive change of coordinates: the physical state stays fixed. A fusion tree is a diagram of the pair grouping and intermediate charges that specify such a basis. Its branches do not depict trajectories or a history of measurements. See Trebst et al., §§2.3–2.4 for the fusion-space construction.

Why can the two pair-charge probabilities differ if the state is unchanged?

They refer to different pair-charge measurements. Let \(O_L\) and \(O_R\) represent the same physical observable in the two bases. Changing coordinates requires \(O_R=FO_LF^\dagger\), so \(c_R^\dagger O_Rc_R=c_L^\dagger O_Lc_L\). The projector onto pair-12 vacuum charge is \(\operatorname{diag}(1,0)\) in the left basis; the projector onto pair-23 vacuum charge has that diagonal form in the right basis. They are different operators. Neither measurement is actually performed by the illustration.

Exchange positions 2 and 3

Let \(R\) be the diagonal matrix of exchange phases in channel order \((1,\tau)\). Let \(B_1\) and \(B_2\) exchange positions 1–2 and 2–3, respectively, both expressed in the left basis. We use the right-handed Fibonacci convention of Bseiso et al., §II.1, Eqs. (2)–(3): positive exchange means counterclockwise, viewed from above the plane, with

\[R=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(e^{-4\pi i/5},e^{3\pi i/5}\right),\qquad B_1=R,\qquad B_2=F^{-1}RF.\]

The two mirror-image Fibonacci theories have opposite chirality: reversing spatial orientation conjugates their exchange phases. The theory used here has topological twist \(T_\tau=e^{4\pi i/5}\), the phase of a \(2\pi\) self-rotation; the vacuum has \(T_1=1\). For a pair with definite total charge \(c\in\{1,\tau\}\), let \(R_c\) denote its exchange phase. Two exchanges obey \((R_c)^2=T_c/T_\tau^2\). Thus the exchange has a channel-dependent phase; it is not simply the twist of one anyon.

Clockwise exchange uses the inverse matrix. The two F-moves below only change coordinates; the central R step is the physical exchange. A prime on \(c'_R\) or \(c'_L\) means the amplitudes after this exchange, in the indicated basis.

\[c_L\ \xrightarrow{\text{change basis }F}\ c_R\ \xrightarrow{\text{exchange }R^{\pm1}}\ c'_R\ \xrightarrow{\text{change back }F^{-1}}\ c'_L.\]

Order, equality, and a measurement

These exchange matrices do not all commute; this is non-Abelian statistics. The braid relation \(B_1B_2B_1=B_2B_1B_2\) does not say that adjacent exchanges commute. Compare short sequences below. The listed order is chronological: \([1,2]\) applies \(B_1\) first and has matrix \(B_2B_1\). A minus sign reverses the exchange direction. Let \(U_A,U_B\) be the product matrices for sequences A and B, and \(|\psi_A\rangle,|\psi_B\rangle\) their normalized output states. The readout compares \(|\langle\psi_A|\psi_B\rangle|^2\) and the matrix difference \(\|U_A-U_B\|_F\), where the Frobenius norm is the square root of the sum of squared entry magnitudes. Why learn this recoupling calculation?

The fusion-path count gives the same golden ratio: the number of allowed states grows asymptotically by the quantum dimension \(d_\tau=\varphi\) per added anyon. It does not assign a noninteger local Hilbert space to a particle.

A qubit stores quantum information in a chosen two-dimensional state space. A quantum gate is a unitary operation on the space used to store that information. A collection of gates is universal if its finite products can approximate any desired unitary on that space, up to a global phase, arbitrarily accurately. Non-Abelian statistics alone does not guarantee this. Ising braiding on its usual qubit encoding generates only Clifford operations, which map products of Pauli matrices to other Pauli products under conjugation. Fibonacci braids can approximate arbitrary encoded unitary gates. This is an approximation statement, not exact synthesis by a finite sequence. The density result is due to Freedman, Larsen, and Wang; Hormozi et al. give methods for finding such sequences. Hormozi et al. assign the displayed numerical \(R\) to clockwise exchange; check orientation conventions before comparing sequences.

An anyon chain is an ordered collection of anyons with interactions between neighbors. An interaction can assign different energies to the possible total charges of a neighboring pair; its Hamiltonian is then a weighted sum of projectors onto those channels. The same F-matrices express each pair's projector in a common many-anyon basis, making the Hamiltonian a matrix we can diagonalize. Feiguin et al.'s golden chain is a concrete example.
