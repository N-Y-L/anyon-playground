Calculate a geometric phase

I want to calculate a Berry phase: the geometric phase acquired when a normalized eigenstate follows a closed, slow variation of its Hamiltonian, after removing the dynamical phase. Diagonalizing the Hamiltonian separately at each point gives eigenvectors with arbitrary overall phases. A product of neighboring overlaps around the closed loop will cancel these arbitrary choices.

Consider a spin \(1/2\) with Hamiltonian \(H=-(\Delta/2)\boldsymbol n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\). Here \(\Delta>0\) is the energy separation of its two eigenstates, \(\boldsymbol\sigma=(\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z)\) are the Pauli matrices, and \(\boldsymbol n\) is a unit vector. The ground state has spin expectation along \(\boldsymbol n\); these directions form the Bloch sphere. Let \(\beta\) be the polar angle from \(+z\) and \(\phi\) the azimuth from \(+x\). In the \(\sigma_z\) basis, its normalized two-component state, or spinor, is \(|u\rangle\):

\[\boldsymbol n=(\sin\beta\cos\phi,\sin\beta\sin\phi,\cos\beta),\qquad |u\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\beta/2)\\e^{i\phi}\sin(\beta/2)\end{pmatrix}.\]

Hold \(\beta\) fixed and take \(\phi\) once around the circle. Positive motion increases \(\phi\), counterclockwise when viewed from \(+z\). The oriented solid angle \(\Omega\) is the enclosed area on the unit sphere, positive for this direction: \(\Omega=\pm2\pi(1-\cos\beta)\). For transport slow enough to remain in the ground state, the Berry phase is \(\gamma=-\Omega/2\), modulo \(2\pi\). The separate dynamical phase is \(-\int E\,dt/\hbar\), with instantaneous ground energy \(E=-\Delta/2\). We calculate only the geometric contribution. Berry’s original paper derives the spin result.

The calculation samples \(N\) equally spaced directions, indexed by \(j=0,\ldots,N-1\). A link joins neighboring samples, with the last returning to the first. The product of their normalized overlap phases is the unit complex number \(W_N\); its argument gives the sampled phase \(\gamma_N=\arg W_N\). The control \(g\) changes only the arbitrary phase chosen for each eigenvector. The formulas below make this cancellation explicit. “Unwrapped” means that the exact angle is displayed without reducing it modulo \(2\pi\); the circular error is the smallest absolute difference modulo \(2\pi\).

Phases differing by \(2\pi\) give the same multiplier. At the south pole the displayed smooth phase is \(\mp2\pi\), equivalent to zero; at the equator either principal value \(+\pi\) or \(-\pi\) represents \(-1\). The error compares the phase factors, not a naive difference of displayed angles.

Watch the arbitrary phases cancel

Write \(|u_j\rangle\) for the normalized eigenvector at sample \(j\). For neighboring states with nonzero overlap, retain only its phase, denoted \(z_j\). The last link returns to the first stored state:

\[z_j=\frac{\langle u_{j+1}|u_j\rangle}{|\langle u_{j+1}|u_j\rangle|},\qquad W_N=\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}z_j,\qquad u_N=u_0,\qquad \gamma_N=\arg W_N.\]

Choose arbitrary real phase angles \(\alpha_j\). Replacing \(|u_j\rangle\) by \(e^{i\alpha_j}|u_j\rangle\) changes a link by \(z_j\mapsto e^{i(\alpha_j-\alpha_{j+1})}z_j\). Each angle appears once with each sign, so their contributions cancel in \(W_N\). Increasing \(g\) changes the individual links while leaving their product fixed. This is the cancellation used in numerical calculations with normalized overlap links.

Why does the overlap product give the geometric phase?

For the displayed spinor at fixed β, a small azimuthal step gives a link whose phase is \(-\sin^2(\beta/2)\,d\phi\) to first order:

\[\begin{aligned}\langle u(\phi+d\phi)|u(\phi)\rangle&=1-i\sin^2(\beta/2)\,d\phi+O(d\phi^2),\\ \gamma&=-\oint\sin^2(\beta/2)\,d\phi=-\frac{\Omega}{2}\pmod{2\pi}.\end{aligned}\]

Multiplying the normalized links adds these infinitesimal phases in the continuum limit; local rephasings still cancel around the closed circuit.

What exactly is the finite-sample error?

With g = 0, every link before normalization is \(\cos^2(\beta/2)+\sin^2(\beta/2)e^{\mp2\pi i/N}\). Its normalized phase raised to the Nth power gives the sampled result. At finite N, the shorter great-circle arcs between spin directions enclose a different area from the smooth latitude. Refinement makes these paths agree. Changing g cannot repair a coarse mesh: it only changes the description.

The error shown is \(|\arg(e^{i\gamma_N}e^{-i\gamma})|\). Neighboring orthogonal states make a normalized link undefined; the calculation rejects such a link rather than assigning it zero phase. The sample counts offered here avoid that singular case for these latitudes.

For reproducibility, the phase choices are \(\alpha_j=g[\sin(2\pi j/N)+0.37\cos(4\pi j/N)]\). Angles and g are in radians. The JSON export records every link and the parameters; no random seed is needed.

What this teaches us about anyons

These loops on the sphere can all be contracted, yet changing their area changes the phase. Geometric does not mean topological. A topological braid contribution remains unchanged under smooth path deformations that keep the excitations separated and the system in the same phase of matter. An anyon calculation must identify the relevant low-energy states and separate that contribution from geometric and dynamical phases that vary with the path.

If several eigenstates share one energy, slow transport can mix them through a unitary matrix, as in Wilczek and Zee’s construction. Such mixing alone does not establish anyons. Non-Abelian anyons have a collective fusion space of states even at fixed particle types, positions, and total topological charge. The energy separation from unwanted excitations and a small splitting among these collective states impose different constraints on transport: it must avoid exciting the former without resolving the latter. Quantitative timing also depends on the path and transition matrix elements. The microscopic braiding analysis by Cheng, Galitski, and Das Sarma discusses these conditions.
