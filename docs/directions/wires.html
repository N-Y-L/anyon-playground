End modes in a finite wire

A finite Kitaev chain · End profiles and parity switches

In the anyon notes, nonlocal information appeared in a collective state space. The two ends of a superconducting wire provide another example of information that is difficult to assign to one small region. If the ends carry protected modes, why can the wire still distinguish two states by their energy? The second boundary is essential to the answer.

The first figure calculates that finite energy splitting and the two spatial parts of its lowest mode. A zero-energy profile constructed from the left end provides a separate check: it must also obey the boundary condition at the right. These are related calculations, but they answer different questions. A second figure examines whether the end solution survives a nonuniform potential.

Each site operator decomposes into two Hermitian operators,

aj=cj+cj†,bj=i(cj†−cj),{aj,ak}={bj,bk}=2δjk.a_j=c_j+c_j^\dagger,\qquad b_j=i(c_j^\dagger-c_j), \qquad \{a_j,a_k\}=\{b_j,b_k\}=2\delta_{jk}.aj​=cj​+cj†​,bj​=i(cj†​−cj​),{aj​,ak​}={bj​,bk​}=2δjk​.

These definitions are possible for every fermion site; by themselves they do not establish an end mode. A zero-energy end mode is a particular linear combination that commutes with the Hamiltonian and is localized near a boundary. That distinction is central to Kitaev's original construction.

For an infinite uniform chain, with lattice spacing set to one, the nonnegative bulk excitation energy is

E(k)=(μ+2wcos⁡k)2+4Δ2sin⁡2k.E(k)=\sqrt{(\mu+2w\cos k)^2+4\Delta^2\sin^2 k}.E(k)=(μ+2wcosk)2+4Δ2sin2k​.

Decay and oscillation

For vj∝zjv_j\propto z^jvj​∝zj, the recurrence gives two roots obeying

Which observations depend on an oscillation, and which only require localization? Hegde and Vishveshwara's study makes this distinction concrete. Their transfer-matrix treatment connects these profiles to finite-chain parity switches and extends the analysis to disorder.

Exact degeneracy in a finite wire

Within the oscillatory interval, the far-end condition requires sin⁡[(N+1)q]=0\sin[(N+1)q]=0sin[(N+1)q]=0. Reindexing the allowed integers gives the complete set

μp=2w2−Δ2cos⁡pπN+1,p=1,…,N.\mu_p=2\sqrt{w^2-\Delta^2}\cos\frac{p\pi}{N+1}, \qquad p=1,\ldots,N.μp​=2w2−Δ2​cosN+1pπ​,p=1,…,N.

These are the black ticks. They are exact zero-mode locations for this uniform finite Hamiltonian, not estimates of its whole low-energy spectrum. At each location an aaa-type solution and a reflected bbb-type solution combine into a zero-energy fermionic degree of freedom. Occupying it changes parity without changing energy. Passing through a simple crossing reverses which parity has the lower energy.

An odd NNN supplies an immediate check: it admits p=(N+1)/2p=(N+1)/2p=(N+1)/2, hence a crossing at μ=0\mu=0μ=0; an even NNN does not. Increasing the chain length changes the allowed boundary phases and adds crossings; it does not move the bulk transition.

The parity reported here labels the lowest-energy state among both parity sectors. An isolated system governed by a parity-conserving Hamiltonian cannot jump between those sectors just because their energies cross. This distinction controls the quench calculation.

The associated localization problem

For a potential that varies by site, the same substitution vj=ρjψjv_j=\rho^j\psi_jvj​=ρjψj​ removes the asymmetric coefficients:

This is a zero-energy equation for an associated normal tight-binding chain. Define its nonnegative growth exponent per site as γN\gamma_NγN​, and define the positive pairing-induced decay rate as γS=−log⁡ρ\gamma_S=-\log\rhoγS​=−logρ. The original profile's leading exponent is γN−γS\gamma_N-\gamma_SγN​−γS​: the end solution decays when γN<γS\gamma_N<\gamma_SγN​<γS​.

For a uniform potential, γN=0\gamma_N=0γN​=0 inside the associated normal band and γN=arcosh⁡(∣μ∣/[2w2−Δ2])\gamma_N=\operatorname{arcosh}(|\mu|/[2\sqrt{w^2-\Delta^2}])γN​=arcosh(∣μ∣/[2w2−Δ2​]) outside it. Three distinct statements: the infinite bulk is topological; a semi-infinite end solution decays; a particular finite wire has an exact zero mode. The boundary condition determines when the third statement follows. The wire calculation alone does not enact an exchange of modes or establish a braiding operation.
