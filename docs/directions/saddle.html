Directions to think about / One particle at a saddle

One particle at a saddle

I start with one particle near a saddle of the potential energy, a stationary point where the energy curves upward in one direction and downward in another. Removing the second particle from the two-anyon calculation removes exchange, but the packet can still stretch, split and lose overlap with its initial shape. The same unstable dynamics also appears in barrier scattering and in wave equations near a black hole. I want to understand how far those connections go.

I retain the lowest Landau level, freezing the particle's cyclotron motion and describing its guiding center, the center of the orbit, by coordinates X,YX,YX,Y. For charge qqq in a field of magnitude B0B_0B0​, the magnetic length is ℓ=ℏ/(∣q∣B0)\ell=\sqrt{\hbar/(|q|B_0)}ℓ=ℏ/(∣q∣B0​)​. I choose the field orientation so that [X,Y]=iℓ2[X,Y]=i\ell^2[X,Y]=iℓ2.

The initial coherent state is a Gaussian with equal uncertainties in X,YX,YX,Y and the minimum allowed uncertainty product. Its means are X0=ℓx0X_0=\ell x_0X0​=ℓx0​ and Y0=ℓy0Y_0=\ell y_0Y0​=ℓy0​, defining the dimensionless displacement labels x0,y0x_0,y_0x0​,y0​. I write ΔX,ΔY\Delta X,\Delta YΔX,ΔY for the standard deviations; initially both are ℓ/2\ell/\sqrt2ℓ/2​. The figure uses dimensionless time τ=λt\tau=\lambda tτ=λt, where ttt is time and λ>0\lambda>0λ>0 is the stretching rate derived below.

I move the packet toward the incoming XXX axis and watch the outgoing split become more even. Then I center it exactly on the saddle: its mean stays put while its shape keeps changing. A stationary mean need not mean a stationary state.

One saddle, two conjugate coordinates

Within the lowest-Landau-level description, the guiding-center coordinates obey

[X,Y]=iℓ2.[X,Y]=i\ell^2.[X,Y]=iℓ2.

They behave as a canonical pair, even though both originated as spatial coordinates. Let g>0g>0g>0 set the saddle curvature, with units of energy per length squared. The projected Hamiltonian HHH and stretching rate λ\lambdaλ are

H=−g2(XY+YX),λ=gℓ2ℏ>0.H=-\frac g2(XY+YX),\qquad \lambda=\frac{g\ell^2}{\hbar}>0.H=−2g​(XY+YX),λ=ℏgℓ2​>0.

Symmetrizing XYXYXY makes the Hamiltonian Hermitian. The Heisenberg equations give

X˙=−λX,X(t)=e−τX(0),Y˙=λY,Y(t)=eτY(0).\begin{aligned} \dot X&=-\lambda X,&X(t)&=e^{-\tau}X(0),\\ \dot Y&=\lambda Y,&Y(t)&=e^\tau Y(0). \end{aligned}X˙Y˙​=−λX,=λY,​X(t)Y(t)​=e−τX(0),=eτY(0).​

One direction contracts while the other expands. Their product and commutator remain unchanged. The rate comes from the second derivatives of the potential energy at the saddle.

Squeezing is this reciprocal contraction and expansion of quantum widths. The ellipse shows guiding-center uncertainties, not a joint measurement of the noncommuting X,YX,YX,Y coordinates. No probability is lost.

Exponential separation alone does not establish chaos. This linear flow can be solved exactly and reversed. It is an instability without the repeated stretching and folding characteristic of chaotic bounded motion.

I can instead ask whether the state matches a fixed Gaussian ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ centered at the origin, with guiding-center variances ℓ2/2\ell^2/2ℓ2/2. A stationary beam asks a third question

A scattering state of definite energy EEE poses a different problem from a localized Gaussian. Here EEE is measured relative to the saddle, omitting the constant cyclotron energy. The notation +X+X+X means the incoming arm on the positive XXX side, and +Y+Y+Y the outgoing arm on the positive YYY side. The conditional probability of that exit is

P(+Y∣+X,E)=11+e2πE/(ℏλ).P(+Y\mid +X,E)=\frac{1}{1+e^{2\pi E/(\hbar\lambda)}}.P(+Y∣+X,E)=1+e2πE/(ℏλ)1​.

This has the form of the equilibrium occupation probability of a fermionic energy level, with chemical potential at the saddle energy. The temperature parameter would be Teff=ℏλ/(2πkB)T_{\mathrm{eff}}=\hbar\lambda/(2\pi k_B)Teff​=ℏλ/(2πkB​), where kBk_BkB​ is Boltzmann's constant. Here it is a scale in a one-particle scattering formula; no many-particle statistics has entered. A pure Gaussian remains pure, and its energy spread cannot be replaced by its mean energy in this stationary formula.

How far does the black-hole connection go?

Near the top of a smooth barrier, the potential is approximately a constant minus a positive multiple of position squared. This is the inverted harmonic oscillator. Waves move away from its unstable maximum. Requiring waves to leave the barrier region, without a continuing incident wave, gives decaying resonant solutions.

Resonance poles

Analytically continuing the scattering amplitude to complex energy reveals poles, called resonance poles, at

En=−iℏλ(n+1/2),n=0,1,….E_n=-i\hbar\lambda(n+1/2),\qquad n=0,1,\ldots.En​=−iℏλ(n+1/2),n=0,1,….

Under outgoing boundary conditions, a pole contributes an amplitude decaying as e−λ(n+1/2)te^{-\lambda(n+1/2)t}e−λ(n+1/2)t. These resonances are not normalizable eigenstates with complex energies. The Hamiltonian still generates unitary evolution; an outgoing signal can decay as probability leaves the observed mode or region. The coherent-mode overlap above has the leading probability rate λ\lambdaλ, but one such curve does not establish the whole pole spectrum.

In a black-hole wave problem, the effective radial barrier can be approximated near its maximum by an inverted oscillator. This gives an approximation to quasinormal modes: decaying wave solutions that leave toward infinity and enter the horizon. Their contribution to the decaying oscillation after a disturbance is called ringdown. Hegde, Subramanyan, Bradlyn and Vishveshwara (2019) develop this connection. Ringdown exists for classical waves; it does not require Hawking radiation, the quantum radiation associated with a black-hole horizon.

For a connection to quantum radiation, the state matters. Stone (2013) fills a set of incoming fermion states to form a Fermi sea in a quantum Hall model. The outgoing particles and holes—missing occupations in that sea—have correlated partners. Tracing out partner modes produces a thermal reduced state. Andrade e Silva and Jacobson (2026) revisit the derivation and its relation to the Unruh effect, the thermal response of a uniformly accelerated detector to the vacuum in flat spacetime. These state and observation choices provide the content behind the thermal description.

Returning to the pair

For an ordinary oscillator, a Hamiltonian made from quadratic combinations of creation and annihilation operators preserves the even and odd occupation sectors: states with even occupation can mix with one another but cannot become odd. This connects oscillator algebra to the pair calculation.

For two anyons, their separation and relative angle form the relative-coordinate problem. The exchange rule restricts its angular states. I must then specify their amplitudes and determine which effective operators represent the physical potential and measurement. Adding an exchange phase to this one-particle Gaussian does not supply those ingredients.

Simple unstable dynamics can connect quantum transport to broader wave physics. I want to understand which features survive changes of state and measurement, and which need extra ingredients such as a filled sea or inaccessible partner modes. This is a way into quantum analogues of gravitational phenomena without equating a thermal-looking curve with Hawking radiation.
