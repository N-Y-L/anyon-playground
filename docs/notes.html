An introduction to anyons

Exchange two identical particles, then exchange them again in the same direction. Their positions are restored. Must their quantum state be restored too? For ordinary bosonic or fermionic statistics the two exchange factors multiply to +1+1+1. In two dimensions there are further possibilities. Anyons are particlelike excitations with statistical rules beyond ordinary bosons and fermions: exchanges can produce other phases, or act on several collective states. Different excitation types can also have nontrivial winding phases with one another.

The background assumed here is graduate mathematics and basic graduate quantum mechanics: states, operators, superposition and adiabatic evolution. Condensed-matter concepts are introduced as needed. The main text develops the physical argument; expandable sections give its derivations. The later transport calculations examine preparation and detection in more detail.

Why two dimensions matter

I keep the particles well separated and move each to the other's starting point. This motion is an exchange. Interchanging their names on paper is only a relabeling; it says nothing about how they moved. Drawing their trajectories against time gives worldlines. A braid records these paths up to smooth deformations that avoid collisions and preserve their endpoints.

For two particles, follow the relative vector r=r1−r2\mathbf r=\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2r=r1​−r2​. Since the particles are identical, r\mathbf rr and −r-\mathbf r−r describe the same unordered configuration. An elementary counterclockwise exchange turns this vector through π\piπ. A second exchange in the same direction makes a full 2π2\pi2π turn; reversing the first motion gives zero net turn. In the plane, the full turn cannot be continuously shrunk away without passing through r=0\mathbf r=0r=0, a collision. In three dimensions the relative direction can leave the plane, and that loop can shrink. The path figure contrasts two exchanges with an exchange followed by its inverse.

This is a statement about the paths. To connect it to statistics, first separate phases caused by ordinary dynamics and applied fields. Energy and elapsed time give a dynamical phase; a charged particle encircling magnetic flux acquires an Aharonov–Bohm phase. The remaining statistical action is called topological when it depends only on the braid, not on the precise path shape. Deforming a path can change those other phases even while its statistical action stays fixed.

Suppose one counterclockwise exchange acts by the scalar U=eiθU=e^{i\theta}U=eiθ, with exchange angle θ\thetaθ defined modulo 2π2\pi2π. In three dimensions the contractible double exchange requires U2=1U^2=1U2=1, hence U=+1U=+1U=+1 for bosons or −1-1−1 for fermions. In two dimensions there is no such requirement. One exchange, two exchanges in the same direction, and an exchange followed by its inverse give, respectively,

U=eiθ,U2=e2iθ,U−1U=1.U=e^{i\theta},\qquad U^2=e^{2i\theta},\qquad U^{-1}U=1.U=eiθ,U2=e2iθ,U−1U=1.

For example, a semion has U=iU=iU=i: two exchanges give −1-1−1, although an exchange and its inverse still cancel. Scalar statistical operations commute and are called Abelian. Later, exchanges will act by matrices that need not commute. The configuration-space argument goes back to Leinaas and Myrheim (1977).

Two dimensions permit these rules; they do not guarantee a material realizes them. Nor is θ\thetaθ generally a continuously adjustable property of one material: different angles can describe different phases of matter or different excitations. We need a microscopic state, an object that can move in it, and a measurement of the resulting transformation.

What is moving?

A phonon is one quantum of a collective atomic vibration. A localized one-phonon packet carries energy through a solid, although no atom travels with it. What moves is a pattern of extra displacement fluctuations; the mean displacement can remain zero. The particle description applies to this pattern in the solid's quantum state.

An excitation is a change of state that carries energy above the ground state. When it persists long enough to move and interact as a particle, it is called a quasiparticle. The description must remain valid during the motion or scattering being studied.

An important realization uses a two-dimensional electron fluid: interacting electrons with approximately uniform density in the interior and no crystalline order. Each electron has charge −e-e−e, with e>0e>0e>0, and obeys fermionic statistics. A localized rearrangement of this fluid can carry a different charge and obey a different exchange rule. These rearrangements are the objects being exchanged. A spin system can also support anyons; the next example obtains a winding phase directly from Pauli operators.

Derive a winding phase from spins

Anyons can also arise in a model containing only localized spins. In Kitaev's toric code, put a spin 1/21/21/2 on every edge ℓ\ellℓ of a square lattice. Its Pauli operators obey XℓZℓ=−ZℓXℓX_\ell Z_\ell=-Z_\ell X_\ellXℓ​Zℓ​=−Zℓ​Xℓ​; operators on different edges commute. The four edges meeting at a vertex sss form its star. An elementary square is a plaquette ppp, with boundary ∂p\partial p∂p. Define products acting on these groups of spins and an energy that favors their +1+1+1 eigenvalues:

As=∏ℓ∋sXℓ,Bp=∏ℓ∈∂pZℓ,H=−Je∑sAs−Jm∑pBp,Je,Jm>0.\begin{gathered} A_s=\prod_{\ell\ni s}X_\ell,\qquad B_p=\prod_{\ell\in\partial p}Z_\ell,\\ H=-J_e\sum_s A_s-J_m\sum_p B_p, \qquad J_e,J_m>0. \end{gathered}As​=ℓ∋s∏​Xℓ​,Bp​=ℓ∈∂p∏​Zℓ​,H=−Je​s∑​As​−Jm​p∑​Bp​,Je​,Jm​>0.​

A star and a plaquette share zero or two edges. Their anticommutation signs therefore cancel: every AsA_sAs​ commutes with every BpB_pBp​. On a lattice with periodic boundaries, a ground state has all these eigenvalues +1+1+1. The positive constants Je,JmJ_e,J_mJe​,Jm​ have units of energy. A vertex with As=−1A_s=-1As​=−1 carries an excitation of type eee; a plaquette with Bp=−1B_p=-1Bp​=−1 carries type mmm, costing 2Je2J_e2Je​ and 2Jm2J_m2Jm​, respectively. On the periodic lattice each type must be created in pairs. Here e,me,me,m label excitation types, not electrical charges. Kitaev's original construction supplies this exactly solvable Hamiltonian.

Applying ZℓZ_\ellZℓ​ flips the two adjacent star eigenvalues. A product of ZZZ operators along an open lattice path—a string operator—flips every interior star twice, leaving only its endpoints excited. Extending the string moves an endpoint; applying it again annihilates the pair. The energy depends on the endpoints, not their separation: these excitations are deconfined. Separating them does not cost an ever-growing energy. Products of XXX similarly create and move mmm pairs along the dual lattice, whose links connect neighboring plaquette centers across their shared edges.

Now move an eee around the boundary of a region RRR of plaquettes. Its closed string is

We(∂R)=∏ℓ∈∂RZℓ=∏p∈RBp.W_e(\partial R)=\prod_{\ell\in\partial R}Z_\ell =\prod_{p\in R}B_p.We​(∂R)=ℓ∈∂R∏​Zℓ​=p∈R∏​Bp​.

The second equality follows because each interior edge occurs twice and Zℓ2=1Z_\ell^2=1Zℓ2​=1; only boundary edges survive. For a simultaneous eigenstate ∣Ψ⟩|\Psi\rangle∣Ψ⟩ with NmN_mNm​ enclosed mmm excitations,

We(∂R)∣Ψ⟩=(−1)Nm∣Ψ⟩.W_e(\partial R)|\Psi\rangle=(-1)^{N_m}|\Psi\rangle.We​(∂R)∣Ψ⟩=(−1)Nm​∣Ψ⟩.

One enclosed mmm therefore gives a minus sign. Equivalently, the ZZZ loop anticommutes with an XXX string that crosses it once. Deforming the loop across unexcited plaquettes multiplies it only by operators with eigenvalue +1+1+1. The phase depends on the parity of enclosed mmm's, not the loop shape. This is mutual statistics, measured by winding one type around another; an interference reference makes the sign observable.

Bringing excitations together gives their fusion rules:

Here 111 is the vacuum type, and ϵ\epsilonϵ is the combined type of an eee and an mmm. Fusion specifies the total excitation type, also called topological charge; it does not require a bound state. Fusing to vacuum can release energy into ordinary excitations, while leaving no nontrivial topological charge. Direct same-type exchanges give +1+1+1 for both eee and mmm: their self-statistics are bosonic. Exchanging two ϵ\epsilonϵ composites additionally includes one mutual winding, giving −1-1−1, so ϵ\epsilonϵ is fermionic. Kitaev's exchange calculation, §5.2, establishes this distinction. Make a hole without removing an electron

The main example is a Laughlin fluid, a correlated state of electrons in a strong perpendicular magnetic field. Its interior, called the bulk, has an excitation gap: a nonzero minimum energy to create additional bulk excitations. It is incompressible because changing the bulk charge density requires creating such excitations. Low-energy motion can still occur at the boundary.

The circular motion of a charged particle in a magnetic field is its cyclotron motion. Quantum mechanically its energies form Landau levels, with many possible orbit centers at each energy. The filling ν\nuν counts electrons per available orbital in one level. At ν=1/3\nu=1/3ν=1/3, the lowest level has roughly one electron per three orbitals. All those orbitals have the same kinetic energy. The electrons can therefore change their correlations to reduce repulsion without paying additional cyclotron energy.

Take NNN electrons with the same fixed spin and positions zi=xi+iyiz_i=x_i+iy_izi​=xi​+iyi​. Choose a field of magnitude B0>0B_0>0B0​>0 into the page. The magnetic length ℓB=ℏ/(eB0)\ell_B=\sqrt{\hbar/(eB_0)}ℓB​=ℏ/(eB0​)​ sets the width of the lowest-level orbitals. With CCC a normalization constant, Laughlin's trial state is

Ψ3=C∏i<j(zi−zj)3exp⁡ ⁣[−∑i∣zi∣24ℓB2],ℓB=ℏeB0.\Psi_3=C\prod_{i<j}(z_i-z_j)^3 \exp\!\left[-\sum_i\frac{|z_i|^2}{4\ell_B^2}\right], \qquad \ell_B=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{eB_0}}.Ψ3​=Ci<j∏​(zi​−zj​)3exp[−i∑​4ℓB2​∣zi​∣2​],ℓB​=eB0​ℏ​​.

As two electrons approach, the cubic factor drives the wavefunction rapidly toward zero. Close pairs become unlikely, reducing their repulsive interaction energy. The odd power also preserves fermionic antisymmetry. For Coulomb interactions this is a trial state representing the Laughlin fluid, rather than a general exact solution.

More generally, the exponent can be an odd integer m≥3m\geq3m≥3 at filling ν=1/m\nu=1/mν=1/m, within the Laughlin liquid regime. The main example will be m=3m=3m=3. Let η\etaη be a chosen complex position in the bulk and multiply the wavefunction by ∏i(zi−η)\prod_i(z_i-\eta)∏i​(zi​−η). The extra zeros suppress electron density near η\etaη, creating a quasihole, while every electron coordinate remains present.

To count the missing electrons, the derivation rewrites the quantum probability density as the statistical weight of a fictitious plasma. That plasma screens an inserted charge: its particles rearrange to cancel the charge's long-distance field. This fixes the missing electron number near the quasihole at 1/m1/m1/m, giving physical charge qh=e/mq_h=e/mqh​=e/m, here +e/3+e/3+e/3. The plasma charges are auxiliary quantities; the quasihole's electrical charge remains. In a finite droplet with fixed NNN, compensating charge lies outside a disk DDD surrounding the quasihole, typically near the edge.

Laughlin (1983) supplies this wavefunction and its charged excitations. The fractional charge is carried by a localized density deficit in a state containing a whole number of electrons.

The parameter η\etaη acquires the meaning of a mobile quasihole position through pinning and transport. A smooth repulsive potential for electrons can pin the deficit. Moving the potential slowly transports a family of low-energy many-electron states with a localized density deficit following it. This works when the bulk gap persists, the quasihole stays away from the edge and other cores, and the motion does not excite the fluid across Δ\DeltaΔ. Removing or weakening the pinning permits superpositions of different positions. An effective wavefunction for the quasihole describes those superpositions. It is a new description of states made from the same electrons, not another elementary constituent added to the Hamiltonian.

What an interferometer has actually measured

A phase multiplying an entire state cannot be observed. Interference supplies a reference. In an ideal two-path experiment, prepare a mobile Abelian anyon in equal amplitudes for orthogonal route states ∣a⟩|a\rangle∣a⟩ and ∣b⟩|b\rangle∣b⟩. Arrange their relative path to encircle a stationary anyon of the same type once counterclockwise. If the remaining relative phase is ϕ\phiϕ, the state just before recombination is

∣χ⟩=∣a⟩+ei(ϕ+2θ)∣b⟩2.|\chi\rangle=\frac{|a\rangle+e^{i(\phi+2\theta)}|b\rangle}{\sqrt2}.∣χ⟩=2​∣a⟩+ei(ϕ+2θ)∣b⟩​.

A balanced recombiner has an output corresponding to ∣+⟩=(∣a⟩+∣b⟩)/2|+\rangle=(|a\rangle+|b\rangle)/\sqrt2∣+⟩=(∣a⟩+∣b⟩)/2​. Its detection probability is

With ϕ=0\phi=0ϕ=0, an empty loop gives P+=1P_+=1P+​=1; a Laughlin quasihole with θ=π/3\theta=\pi/3θ=π/3 inside gives P+=1/4P_+=1/4P+​=1/4. The enclosed quasihole has changed a probability. This ideal comparison assumes the other phases are matched and no unobserved degree of freedom records which route was taken. Losing that coherence removes the interference signal.

Charge can move along the boundary of a Hall fluid even when its bulk is gapped. The low-energy excitations propagate in boundary modes called edge channels. At a narrow constriction, a quasiparticle can tunnel between nearby edges. Two separated constrictions provide alternative paths enclosing part of the fluid; their amplitudes interfere if coherence is maintained. This interferometer measures a current response from many such tunneling events, rather than the single-particle probability just calculated.

Let GoscG_{\rm osc}Gosc​ be the oscillatory part of its conductance, the derivative of mean current with respect to a small applied voltage. At filling 1/31/31/3, the interfering charge is qh=e/3q_h=e/3qh​=e/3. When a phase is no longer enough

Fix the quasihole positions on a disk and hold the edge state fixed. For well-separated Laughlin quasiholes, one low-energy state remains, up to a phase. Other systems can retain several states even when the excitation types and their combined type are fixed. As separation grows, their energies approach equality and local measurements near one excitation cease to distinguish them. Exchanges act within this space by unitary matrices. If different exchanges fail to commute, the excitations are non-Abelian anyons.

A pair's fusion channel is its combined topological charge: the excitation type seen by a probe surrounding both particles, or the type that remains when they are brought together. As in the spin example, it need not be an electric charge. In the Ising anyon model there are types 111 (vacuum, meaning no nontrivial excitation), σ\sigmaσ and ψ\psiψ (a fermionic excitation), with

σ×σ=1+ψ.\sigma\times\sigma=1+\psi.σ×σ=1+ψ.

The plus sign lists allowed pair types, not equal probabilities. The other rules needed here are ψ×ψ=1\psi\times\psi=1ψ×ψ=1 and that vacuum acts as an identity, so 1×1=11\times1=11×1=1 and 1×ψ=ψ1\times\psi=\psi1×ψ=ψ. Four σ\sigmaσ excitations with total type 111 therefore span two states: the first pair and the second pair can both have type 111, or both type ψ\psiψ. A mixed assignment has total type ψ\psiψ and is excluded. I call the two allowed states ∣0⟩,∣1⟩|0\rangle,|1\rangle∣0⟩,∣1⟩. These are collective pair labels, not a spin attached to each excitation.

One exchange can rotate a collective state, and exchanging a different pair can rotate it in a different way. Let B1B_1B1​ exchange the first pair counterclockwise and B2B_2B2​ the middle pair. Starting from ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩, the Ising matrices specified below give, up to an overall phase,

B1B2∣0⟩=∣0⟩+∣1⟩2=∣+⟩,B2B1∣0⟩=∣0⟩−i∣1⟩2.\begin{aligned} B_1B_2|0\rangle&=\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}=|+\rangle,\\ B_2B_1|0\rangle&=\frac{|0\rangle-i|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}. \end{aligned}B1​B2​∣0⟩B2​B1​∣0⟩​=2​∣0⟩+∣1⟩​=∣+⟩,=2​∣0⟩−i∣1⟩​.​

The rightmost operation acts first. Measuring the first pair's fusion channel gives vacuum with probability 1/21/21/2 in either state, so this measurement misses the difference. The middle pair is different: its vacuum channel is ∣+⟩|+\rangle∣+⟩. Projecting onto that channel gives probabilities 111 and 1/21/21/2, respectively. The exchange order has changed a measurable outcome. The Ising figure lets us compose the operations and choose which pair to measure.

For this transport, “slow” means slow enough to avoid exciting states above the collective space. If finite separation weakly splits its states, the braid should also finish before that splitting is resolved dynamically. The gap above the space and the splitting within it therefore constrain transport on different timescales.

Ivanov (2001) obtains noncommuting exchanges in a particular two-dimensional superconducting model. Electrons form a coherent pair state, or condensate, described by a complex amplitude. A vortex is a defect around which that amplitude's phase winds as we follow a closed spatial circuit. The vortices studied in the paper bind Majorana zero modes: localized zero-energy modes represented by Hermitian fermionic operators. Two such operators combine into one ordinary fermion mode, which can be empty or occupied.

The fermion parity records whether the total fermion number is even or odd. Creating or annihilating a pair changes fermion number by two and preserves parity. Four separated vortices have two fermion modes and, at fixed parity, two allowed joint occupations. Their exchange operations act noncommutatively on this two-dimensional space. Ivanov determines the operations up to an overall phase; the paper discussion follows the four-vortex example.

The superconducting model and its Majorana operators

Ivanov's model has pairs with total spin 111 (spin-triplet pairing) and one unit of relative orbital angular momentum (ppp-wave pairing). The pairing is chiral: it selects one sense of orbital rotation. Besides its overall phase, the pair amplitude has a pairing axis along which the pair's spin projection is zero. A half-quantum vortex combines a π\piπ phase winding with a π\piπ rotation of that axis on a circuit around the core, returning the condensate to itself. It is these defects, in this model, that bind the Majorana modes.

What is protected?

A gap makes it costly to create extra excitations. That alone does not protect stored information: independent spins in a magnetic field are gapped, but a local field can still read or rotate one spin. The extra ingredient here is that different collective states look the same to local probes. For well-separated non-Abelian anyons, the information is in how their types combine. A measurement near one anyon cannot determine a distant pair's fusion channel.

The toric code shows a related effect even without anyons present. Join opposite edges of the square lattice to make a torus. A closed ZZZ string that can shrink to a point is a product of plaquette operators and has value +1+1+1 in every ground state. A string around a hole of the torus cannot be reduced this way. Take two ZZZ strings around the two independent cycles: they commute, and their two eigenvalues, each +1+1+1 or −1-1−1, label four ground states, all with the same local star and plaquette values. A local disturbance cannot read which state was chosen; a process extending around the torus can.

Derivation: why the torus has four ground states

For an L×LL\times LL×L square lattice with periodic boundaries, with integer L≥3L\geq3L≥3, there are 2L22L^22L2 spins, L2L^2L2 stars and L2L^2L2 plaquettes. The product of all stars is the identity, because each edge occurs twice. The product of all plaquettes is also the identity. Only L2−1L^2-1L2−1 constraints of each kind are independent. Starting from a Hilbert space of dimension 22L22^{2L^2}22L2, each independent commuting constraint halves the dimension. The ground space therefore has dimension

22L2−(L2−1)−(L2−1)=4.2^{2L^2-(L^2-1)-(L^2-1)}=4.22L2−(L2−1)−(L2−1)=4.

An eee string or an mmm string wrapping a noncontractible cycle acts within this space: it leaves no endpoints, hence creates no excitations. Appropriate crossing strings anticommute and change the global loop values. Strings confined to a small disk cannot do this. Kitaev's construction makes the distinction between local errors and global operations explicit.

This is a characteristic of topological order: a gapped phase with locally indistinguishable collective states on suitable closed surfaces, and excitations with fusion and braiding rules stable under weak local perturbations that do not close the gap. The torus degeneracy and a non-Abelian fusion space are different manifestations. The toric code's anyons are Abelian; its four torus ground states do not make them non-Abelian. Conversely, four non-Abelian anyons can supply a fusion space on an ordinary disk.

At finite separation, a perturbation can couple collective states through virtual processes, giving a small energy splitting. Real unwanted excitations can also travel and change a fusion result or a global loop value. Protection therefore improves with separation and control of excitations; it is not immunity to every error. Smoothly changing a braid's path preserves its statistical action, but performing the wrong braid does not.

For quantum computation, the protected states must also be prepared, transformed and measured. Ising braids supply only a restricted set of transformations; they cannot approximate every unitary operation. Additional operations are required. Nayak and collaborators (2008) explain both the attraction of this approach and the constraints. The toric-code memory calculation illustrates how a local error process can eventually change a global state.

Further directions

One direction begins before any of these measurements: which interactions stabilize an anyon phase, and which combinations of fusion and braiding rules can occur in a physical system? The toric Hamiltonian constructs one answer; the Laughlin state is a trial wavefunction whose phase must survive realistic interactions. Finding and identifying further realizations requires both a candidate many-body state and evidence for its excitations.

The examples also leave three questions about manipulating those excitations. First, how can a detector separate statistics from changes in preparation, path area and interactions? Second, what keeps a collective state protected while it is moved and measured? Third, which excitation types and motions remain possible when the surface or boundary changes? The further directions develop these questions through calculations and papers.

The transport continuation follows one route in detail: prepare two effective anyons, let a saddle potential separate their trajectories, and ask which measured quantity retains information about exchange. A final collider example shows how even one-particle interference can alter a comparison intended to reveal statistics. These calculations supply assumptions that can be changed and observables that can be recalculated.
