1. Exchange is a path, not just a permutation

Imagine moving two identical, well-separated excitations in a plane. An exchange swaps their positions. A second exchange in the same direction returns them to the starting configuration, but the paths have wound around one another. The interesting question is what the state remembers when the particles are back where they started. In two dimensions, the paths can carry information that the final positions do not. Leinaas and Myrheim develop the configuration-space argument behind this distinction.

A braid is the set of particle paths through space and time. In an Abelian anyon model each braid acts by a scalar phase. For two anyons, let \(k\) count counterclockwise exchanges minus clockwise exchanges:

\[U=e^{ik\theta},\qquad k=1\ \text{(exchange)},\quad k=2\ \text{(full winding)}.\]

The exchange angle \(\theta\) sets the self-exchange rule in this illustration; by itself it does not identify an entire anyon type. Bosons have \(\theta=0\), fermions have \(\theta=\pi\), and a semion has \(\theta=\pi/2\). For a microscopic example of fractional statistics, see the Berry-phase calculation for quantum Hall quasiholes by Arovas, Schrieffer, and Wilczek. The circle on the right shows the multiplier for the completed operation; the moving dots only illustrate the path.

Why two dimensions matter

In the plane a full winding cannot be shrunk away without a collision. In three spatial dimensions there is room to disentangle it. For ordinary scalar exchange statistics in three dimensions, doing the exchange twice must therefore act as the identity, giving the familiar \(+1\) or \(-1\). In two dimensions that argument no longer restricts the phase to those two values.

What has been omitted from the phase?

Actual transport can also accumulate dynamical and electromagnetic phases. Here we isolate the statistical contribution. We assume a gapped phase, separated quasiparticles, and motion slow enough to avoid unwanted excitations. 2. Turn a statistical phase into a probability

The phase in the previous note cannot be read off from a lone state. Give it something to interfere with. Split a probe into two coherent alternatives and recombine them. Let one alternative wind \(w\) times around \(N\) anyons of the same Abelian type as the probe. Relative to the reference route, this adds the statistical phase \(\delta=2Nw\theta\). Write the remaining controllable relative phase as \(\phi\), so \(\Delta=\phi+\delta\).

\[P_0=\frac{1+V\cos\Delta}{2},\qquad P_1=1-P_0.\]

The two paths are balanced. Their visibility \(0\le V\le1\) measures the retained coherence: \(V=1\) gives full-contrast fringes and \(V=0\) gives no interference. The dotted curve removes the enclosed anyons while keeping the other settings fixed. Try changing the reference phase until a previously dark output becomes bright; that is how a relative phase becomes a measurable probability. The fractional-statistics contribution has a microscopic origin in Arovas et al.; a real interferometer involves further physics, as in Nakamura et al.

Where the cosine comes from

For \(V=1\), choose the recombiner convention \(H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\). Writing the two orthogonal route states as \(|a\rangle\) and \(|b\rangle\), the incoming path state is \((|a\rangle+e^{i\Delta}|b\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Applying \(H\) gives

\[A_0=\frac{1+e^{i\Delta}}2,\qquad A_1=\frac{1-e^{i\Delta}}2,\qquad P_j=|A_j|^2.\]

At reduced visibility there is no single pure-state amplitude pair for the ensemble. The phasor picture above is replaced by a density matrix; scaling an amplitude by \(V\) would give the wrong probabilities.

Both coherent and partially coherent cases are described in the path basis by the following density matrix. Recombination gives \(P_j=(H\rho H^\dagger)_{jj}\). Changing \(V\) suppresses off-diagonal coherence while each path population stays at \(1/2\).

3. Non-Abelian exchange: order and measurement

A braid need not act by just one phase. If several anyons share a space of possible fusion states, exchanging them can rotate that state. Now change to the Ising model, where two anyons of type \(\sigma\) can combine to give vacuum \(1\) or the fermionic type \(\psi\): \(\sigma\times\sigma=1+\psi\). This is a fusion rule: the plus sign lists allowed total charges, not a sum of particles or a claim of equal probabilities. Topological charge labels excitation type; it is not electric charge.

Four \(\sigma\) anyons with total vacuum charge have a two-dimensional state space. Choose \(|0\rangle\) when the first pair fuses to \(1\), and \(|1\rangle\) when it fuses to \(\psi\). The other pair must fuse to the same type. The two basis states belong to the same total-charge sector, so their coherent superpositions are allowed.

\[|0\rangle=|((\sigma\sigma)_1(\sigma\sigma)_1)_1\rangle,\qquad |1\rangle=|((\sigma\sigma)_\psi(\sigma\sigma)_\psi)_1\rangle.\]

Exchanging neighbors acts on this shared space by a unitary matrix \(B_j\). The first comparison below applies \(B_1\) and then \(B_2\), versus \(B_2\) and then \(B_1\). The order of matrix multiplication now has an observable consequence. The exchange algebra appears for Majorana vortices in Ivanov's derivation; the fusion-space conventions used here are collected in Nayak et al.

This gives a concrete reason that braiding alone is not a universal gate set. The statement concerns these preparations; it does not say that an arbitrary initial qubit state is forbidden. The mechanism is a pair of quarter-turns of the encoded Bloch vector. Including the common overall phase, the matrices are

\[B_1=B_3=e^{i\pi/8}e^{-i\pi Z/4},\qquad B_2=e^{i\pi/8}e^{-i\pi X/4}.\]

They rotate by \(\pi/2\) about different axes, so their order matters. The common phase does not affect the component bars or measurement probabilities. See Nayak et al. for the computational consequences.

Derive the braid matrices

In the first-pair fusion basis, exchange is diagonal: each possible pair charge gets its own phase. To exchange the middle pair, change to the basis in which that pair's charge is definite, exchange, then change back. The matrix \(F\) performs that change of fusion basis; here \(F^{-1}=F\).

\[B_1=B_3=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\pi/8}&0\\0&e^{3i\pi/8}\end{pmatrix},\qquad F=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]
\[B_2=FB_1F,\qquad |\mathrm{final}\rangle=B_2B_1|\mathrm{initial}\rangle\quad\text{for “1 then 2”.}\]

Clockwise exchanges use \(B_j^\dagger\). Reversing order alone does not undo a sequence; undoing requires reversing order and inverting every exchange. The exact phases and basis convention follow Nayak et al., Sec. II.A.1 and Eq. (50).

This is ideal unitary evolution inside a fixed fusion space. It does not simulate a Majorana device, preparation or readout dynamics, finite-separation splitting, or error correction. Ising braiding alone is not a universal set of quantum gates.

4. A fusion rule builds a Hilbert space

Before trying to store or manipulate a state, count how many independent states are available. One short fusion rule already tells us a surprising amount. Use a different model: Fibonacci anyons have charge types \(1\) and \(\tau\), with

\[1\times\tau=\tau,\qquad \tau\times\tau=1+\tau.\]

Add the anyons one at a time. After each addition, record the cumulative charge. A previous charge \(1\) must become \(\tau\); a previous charge \(\tau\) can become \(1\) or \(\tau\). One allowed chain of intermediate charges specifies a basis vector. It is not a trajectory in real space or a claim that a sequence of measurements happened. The connection between this counting and quantum gates is developed in Bonesteel et al., Fig. 1.

Count without listing every path

Let \(a_n\) count paths ending in vacuum and \(b_n\) count those ending in \(\tau\). Every path to vacuum comes from \(\tau\); a path to \(\tau\) can come from either type. Therefore

\[a_{n+1}=b_n,\qquad b_{n+1}=a_n+b_n,\qquad(a_0,b_0)=(1,0).\]

At \(n=4\), the dimensions are \(2\) and \(3\). These are Fibonacci numbers. For large \(n\), either fixed-sector dimension grows by a factor approaching \(d_\tau=(1+\sqrt5)/2\) per added anyon. The noninteger quantum dimension describes asymptotic growth; every finite Hilbert-space dimension here is an integer.

What has not been calculated?

The fusion rule counts basis states. It does not determine a state vector, measurement probabilities, or a braid operator. To braid Fibonacci anyons, one also needs the model's \(F\) and \(R\) data. The Ising matrices in the preceding note cannot be reused. The catalog outlines a Fibonacci gate-search extension.

5. Anyons from a solvable spin Hamiltonian

Up to now, we have supplied the exchange and fusion rules. Here we can see them emerge from ordinary spin operators. Place a spin \(1/2\) on each edge of a square lattice. The toric-code Hamiltonian uses products of Pauli operators around a vertex \(s\) and around a square plaquette \(p\):

\[H=-J_e\sum_s A_s-J_m\sum_p B_p,\qquad A_s=\prod_{\ell\ni s}X_\ell,\quad B_p=\prod_{\ell\in\partial p}Z_\ell.\]

Take \(J_e,J_m>0\). The operators \(A_s\) and \(B_p\) commute and have eigenvalues \(\pm1\); a ground state has all eigenvalues \(+1\). A vertex with \(A_s=-1\) is an \(e\) excitation; a plaquette with \(B_p=-1\) is an \(m\) excitation. “Electric” and “magnetic” name these two types, not necessarily literal electric and magnetic charges. This exactly solvable construction is due to Kitaev, Sec. 1.

One \(X_\ell\) flips the two plaquettes sharing that edge: it creates a pair of \(m\)'s, moves one, or annihilates a neighboring pair. Select two neighboring squares below to apply that edge operator. Dashed segments record the applied string of \(X\)'s. Along an unbranched string, each interior plaquette is flipped twice; only its endpoints are flipped once. Starting without excitations, a square carries an \(m\) when an odd number of string edges meet there, including a three-edge junction. For an edited reference configuration, odd string incidence instead toggles that square's reference occupation.

Derive the minus sign

A closed path of \(e\) is a product of \(Z\) operators along the lattice boundary. Multiply all \(B_p\)'s inside that boundary: every interior edge appears twice and cancels because \(Z_\ell^2=1\). Only the boundary remains. Each enclosed \(m\) has \(B_p=-1\), so

\[W_e(\partial R)=\prod_{p\in R}B_p,\qquad W_e|\Psi\rangle=(-1)^{N_m}|\Psi\rangle.\]

Here \(N_m\) counts the enclosed \(m\) excitations in the specified eigenstate \(|\Psi\rangle\). Repeating the loop \(w\) times gives \((-1)^{N_mw}\). This is mutual statistics. Both \(e\) and \(m\) have bosonic self-exchange, yet an \(e\) winding around an \(m\) gives \(-1\). Equivalently, a direct-lattice \(Z\) loop and a dual-lattice \(X\) string anticommute when they cross an odd number of times; even crossings commute. Their composite \(\epsilon=e\times m\) is fermionic.

What this patch represents, and what it leaves out

The drawing tracks magnetic plaquette eigenvalues and the parity of applied \(X\) strings exactly; it does not store the entire many-spin wavefunction. Pair mode preserves the parity of displayed \(m\)'s. Individual-occupation mode only edits a configuration; an odd displayed count requires a partner outside the patch. Clearing the picture resets the model rather than simulating a local annihilation process.

The loop is contractible. For a noncontractible loop and logical memory, change the boundary conditions as in note 7. Temperature and decoding require further ingredients. The sign also needs a reference process to become a measured probability, just as in the interferometer. Kitaev's lattice model supplies the microscopic reasoning without requiring quantum field theory.

6. The same anyons can bunch and antibunch

Bosons bunch; fermions antibunch. That familiar slogan suggests putting anyons somewhere between the two. But which observable are we talking about, and how were the particles prepared? For the localized pair below, changing the packet separation can reverse the sign of the statistical correlation while the exchange angle stays fixed. This is the effect in Vishveshwara and Cooper, Fig. 1 and Eqs. (3), (5), (6).

We have changed physical models again. Consider two identical Abelian anyons projected into the lowest Landau level (LLL), the lowest cyclotron-energy level in a magnetic field. The fast cyclotron motion is frozen; the remaining guiding center is the quantum coordinate of the orbit's center. The preparation is the paper's localized two-anyon packet, not an arbitrary state with the same exchange phase.

Write the exchange angle as \(\theta=\pi\alpha\), with \(0\leq\alpha\leq1\). The packet centers have separation \(d\), measured here by \(s=d/\ell\), where \(\ell=\sqrt{\hbar/|qB|}\) is the single-particle magnetic length in SI units, \(q\) is the moving particle's charge, and \(B\) is the perpendicular magnetic field. The plotted quantity compares the mean squared relative guiding-center separation with a distinguishable-particle reference prepared at the same \(s\):

\[\chi(s,\alpha)=\frac{\langle\hat r^2\rangle_\alpha-\langle\hat r^2\rangle_{\mathrm d}}{4\ell^2},\qquad \frac{\langle\hat r^2\rangle_{\mathrm d}}{\ell^2}=s^2+2.\]

Thus \(\chi<0\) means a smaller mean squared separation, called bunching here; \(\chi>0\) means antibunching. The zero line is the distinguishable reference. This is a dimensionless difference of second moments, not a coincidence probability or a normalized \(g^{(2)}\).

Look inside the packet

The allowed relative angular momenta are \(L_k=(2k+\alpha)\hbar\), for \(k=0,1,\ldots\). Localization at a chosen separation requires a superposition of these angular-momentum states. For this preparation, define \(u=s^2/4\); their normalized weights are

\[w_k=\frac{u^{2k+\alpha}}{\Gamma(2k+\alpha+1)},\qquad p_k=\frac{w_k}{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}w_j},\qquad \chi=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k+\alpha)p_k-u.\]

At the formal \(s=0\) limit, \(p_0=1\) gives \(\langle L\rangle=\alpha\hbar\), hence \(\langle\hat r^2\rangle_\alpha/\ell^2=4\alpha+2\), versus \(2\) for the reference. Thus \(\chi=\alpha\) comes from the lowest allowed angular momentum, not a repulsive force. Use this normalized limit rather than a ratio of vanishing weights. Here \(\Gamma\) extends the factorial: \(\Gamma(n+1)=n!\). Increasing the separation redistributes weight among the allowed angular momenta. The shift of their mean relative to \(u\) is precisely the correlation plotted above. The bars are the probabilities for an angular-momentum measurement on this prepared state; they are not probabilities of detecting two particles at the same point.

Check the endpoints and the preparation

The bosonic and fermionic limits reduce to \(\chi(s,0)=u(\tanh u-1)\) and \(\chi(s,1)=u(\coth u-1)\), with the formal values \(0\) and \(1\) at \(u=0\). More generally, \(\chi(0,\alpha)=\alpha\). The displayed finite sum is evaluated to a controlled numerical tolerance.

The normalized relative state in the paper is \(\lvert z\rangle_\alpha\propto\sum_k (z^*/2)^{2k+\alpha}[\Gamma(2k+\alpha+1)]^{-1/2}\lvert k,\alpha\rangle\), with \(|z|=s\). Its preparation matters: the exchange angle alone does not determine a pair-correlation function. The paper goes on to relate these correlations to motion through a saddle potential; the figure here computes the static guiding-center second moment.

7. No defects left—and still a different state

Return to the toric code, but identify opposite edges of the square: leaving the right edge brings a defect back at the left, and likewise for top and bottom. This is a torus drawn as a flat periodic tile. Create two \(m\) excitations, take one around the periodic direction, and annihilate the pair. Every local plaquette can look repaired even though the encoded state has changed. This distinction is the starting point of Kitaev's toric-code memory and Dennis et al.'s analysis of logical errors.

The worked path has two defects until they annihilate, so its energy stays at \(4J_m\), then returns to zero. The final noncontractible string still flips a logical loop eigenvalue. Energy and local excitation checks do not distinguish the degenerate ground states.

Why the local checks miss the change

A small closed \(X\) loop bounds a region. It is a product of star stabilizers, so it acts as the identity on the ground space. A loop that winds around the torus bounds no disk on the torus. It can cross a conjugate noncontractible \(Z\) loop once, and that crossing gives \(XZ=-ZX\). Starting from the reference state, the closed string therefore changes the loop eigenvalues to

\[\langle\overline Z_{\mathrm{vertical}}\rangle=(-1)^{p_x},\qquad\langle\overline Z_{\mathrm{horizontal}}\rangle=(-1)^{p_y}.\]

Local excitation checks and noncontractible loop measurements answer different questions. With no local defects, the four parity pairs label four orthogonal ground states for this chosen initial state. In the twice-around example the same \(X\) cycle is applied twice; the parity returns to zero because each edge operator squares to the identity.

What is tracked, and where error correction would enter

The calculation tracks the exact parity of applied edges, their odd-incidence endpoints, and the winding class of closed strings. It starts from a specified ground state rather than storing all spins' amplitudes. An open string creates excitations, so its current seam-crossing counts are not presented as a ground-space label.

This is a logical-operator demonstration, not a noise model or a decoder. A decoder only sees a measured excitation syndrome, not the actual error string drawn here. Choosing a recovery string in the wrong winding class can remove every defect while leaving a logical error. See Dennis et al., “Topological quantum memory” for that next step.
